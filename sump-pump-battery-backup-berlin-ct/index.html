Sump Pump Battery Backup for Berlin, CT Homes

Storms that fill a sump pit often knock out the power, too. A battery backup is the part that keeps the water moving. This guide explains how it works and what to plan.

Why a primary pump is not enough

A standard sump pump runs on household power. When a storm takes out the lines, the pump stops at the moment the pit fills fastest. A basement can take on water in a few hours. A backup system adds a second pump that runs on a battery or on water pressure, so the pit keeps being emptied.

Backups also cover failures that have nothing to do with outages. A tripped breaker, a failed primary pump or a stuck float can all be covered by a second pump, if it sits at a slightly higher float setting.

Battery systems are the most common choice for homes on public water. Water-powered systems have no battery to replace, but they depend on supply pressure and local rules, so ask your water supplier before choosing one.

Sizing and installation

A backup pump must fit the pit and match the flow of the primary. We check the pit size, the discharge pipe and the check valve. A separate discharge line for the backup avoids sharing a failure point. The battery sits near the pit on a shelf or stand, with a charger plugged into a protected outlet.

An alarm adds a useful warning. A high water alarm tells you the pit is rising before the floor is wet, and some systems send a notice to a phone. Ask what the alarm covers and whether it works without power.

  1. Check the pit and primary pump

    We confirm size, float and check valve condition.

  2. Set the backup float

    The backup pump sits slightly above the primary float level.

  3. Run the discharge

    A second discharge or a tee with a second check valve carries the water out.

  4. Connect and test

    The charger, battery and alarm are tested with the main power off.

Keeping the backup ready

A battery that has gone flat or aged out is a backup in name only. Test the system each season by lifting the float, and watch the pump run. Check the charger light and the battery terminals. Replace the battery on the schedule the maker gives.

Pour a few gallons of water into the pit twice a year to confirm the primary pump starts and the discharge runs clear. Clear debris from the pit. Check the outside discharge for ice in winter.

Wiring and safety

The charger should be on a protected outlet away from standing water. Never use a battery system on a wet floor. If water is near the electrical panel, stay out and call a licensed professional.

A backup does not replace repair. If your primary pump runs constantly or fails often, see sump pump repair first, and consider basement waterproofing if water keeps entering the foundation.

Alarms and monitoring that earn their place

A backup pump that quietly runs for hours is useful only if someone notices. Many systems include an alarm that sounds when the water reaches a high level or when the battery is low. Some connect to a phone app or a home network. Pick the level of monitoring that matches how often you are away.

For a seasonal home or a family that travels, remote alerts are worth the added cost. A text that says the pit is rising can let a neighbor or a plumber get there before the floor is wet. Test the alert each season, because a notice that never reaches your phone is no protection.

Choosing between one pit and two

Many homes have one pit with one pump, and a backup pump can often fit in the same pit if the pit is large enough. A tight pit can force the two pumps to interfere with each other, so the floats tangle or the second pump sits too high to be useful.

In that case a second pit or a larger pit is a better design. It costs more up front, but it avoids a failure mode that only shows itself in the worst storm. We measure the pit before suggesting anything, and we tell you honestly if the existing one is too small.

Storm season checklist

What to do before storm season

  • Pour water into the pit and watch both pumps start.
  • Check the battery charger light and the terminals.
  • Clear debris from the pit and the discharge outlet.
  • Make sure the alarm sounds and the phone alert arrives.
  • Keep stored items off the basement floor.

A five-minute test can save a flooded basement. Write the date on the battery so you know when it was last checked.

Public water and discharge rules in Berlin

A water-powered backup draws from the public water system, and Berlin is served by more than one, according to Connecticut Department of Public Health records. Ask your supplier whether a water-powered unit is allowed on your street.

Where the pump discharges can also involve town rules, so ask the building department before adding a new discharge. We serve basements in Southington and Cromwell too, and the questions are the same.
