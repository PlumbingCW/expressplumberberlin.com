When a plumbing failure shuts the business down

A backed-up floor drain in a kitchen, a burst line above a ceiling or a failed water heater in a salon can stop work immediately. Close off the affected area, shut off the water at the nearest valve if you can, and call the 24/7 line. A licensed plumber will tell you what to do while a crew is arranged.

We work around business hours when we can. After-hours calls keep the shop open the next morning, and we describe the repair before starting so the owner or manager can decide.

Before we arrive

  • Close off affected areas and move customers away.
  • Shut off the nearest fixture valve or the building main.
  • Keep staff out of wet areas near electrical equipment.
  • Note what failed and when.
  • Have the owner or manager available for approval.

Preventive work that keeps a business open

A restaurant can avoid many emergencies with scheduled drain cleaning and grease trap service. A shop with older piping benefits from a camera inspection before a problem becomes an outage. We discuss options after the first call and do not push contracts.

For kitchens, hydro jetting keeps grease lines open. For the main sewer, see sewer line cleaning. Backflow testing and grease trap cleaning are handled as part of this service, and requirements vary by property and municipality, so we confirm them with the town.

Compliance and permits

Commercial plumbing work in Connecticut falls under the State Building Code, which adopted the 2021 International Plumbing Code in the 2022 edition effective October 1, 2022. Work generally needs a licensed plumber, and the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection lets you verify a plumbing license online.

We confirm whether a job needs a permit with the Berlin Building Department before work starts. That protects the owner and the tenant.

Restaurants and food service on the Turnpike corridor

A kitchen puts more load on a plumbing system than almost any other business. Dish machines, prep sinks, floor drains and grease-laden lines work all day, and a single stoppage can close the line. We look at the whole drain path, not just the fixture that failed, because grease that cools in a long run is the usual cause.

Where a grease interceptor is installed, it needs regular pumping and the line to and from it needs cleaning. Requirements for sizing and servicing vary by property and by municipality, so we confirm them with the town rather than guessing. We tell you what we see, and we suggest a service schedule that fits the volume of cooking.

Floor drains in a kitchen also need a trap seal. A dry trap lets sewer gas into the room, and a simple fix, adding water or a trap primer, solves it.

Offices, shops and multi-tenant buildings

In a small office or shop, the usual calls are restroom fixtures, a water heater and a slow floor drain. In a multi-tenant building, the first task is deciding which line serves which unit. A clog in a shared stack shows up in the wrong tenant first, and the owner and the manager need the same information.

We describe the problem to whoever holds the lease or the building, and we keep a short record of what we did. If a tenant needs work that the landlord must approve, we explain what is needed so the approval comes faster.

Planning work around business hours

Whenever possible we schedule shutdowns before opening or after closing, and we tell you in advance when water will be off. For a restaurant, that may mean working overnight. For a shop, it may be early morning. We confirm the plan the day before so staff are not caught off guard.

If the problem is urgent and cannot wait, we say what can be done at once and what needs a second visit. You decide how to run the business while the repair is made.
