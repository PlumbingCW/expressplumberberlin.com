When a failed sump pump is an emergency

A sump pump that stops during a storm can flood a basement within hours. If water is rising in the pit, check that the pump is plugged in and that the breaker has not tripped. Call the 24/7 line. A licensed plumber will tell you what to do while a crew is arranged, including how to move belongings off the floor.

Never stand in water near electrical outlets or equipment. If the water is close to outlets, stay out and leave the breaker alone until it is safe. Move valuables to a higher level and photograph the basement for your insurer.

What to check while you wait

  • Is the pump plugged in and the breaker on?
  • Does the float move freely, or is it stuck against the pit wall?
  • Is the discharge pipe frozen or clogged outside?
  • Does the pump run but not move water?
  • Never wade near outlets or electrical panels.

Most pumps wear out in several years, and a pump that has run continuously through wet seasons wears faster. Debris in the pit, a clogged discharge and a failed float switch are the common causes of a call. A battery backup or water-powered backup keeps the pit working in a power outage.

Replacing or installing a sump pump

A new pump needs the right size for the pit and the flow. A pit that is too small cycles the pump too often. A pump that is too small cannot keep up. We check the pit, the discharge route and the check valve. If there is no pit, we discuss installing one and routing the discharge away from the foundation.

Discharge location matters. Water should go where it does not return to the foundation or flood a neighbor. We check the pipe slope outside and look for freezing risks in winter. Local rules on where sump water can go can differ by town, so we confirm with the Berlin Building Department when the job involves a new discharge route.

Berlin basements and wet weather

Berlin sits in Hartford County, where heavy rain and spring thaw regularly test basements. Whether your property has a high water table depends on the lot, and we do not guess. A pump that cycles often in dry weather can suggest groundwater or a leaking pipe, and a camera or leak check can separate the two.

If you see damp walls or floor cracks even with a working pump, the real problem may be water entering through the foundation. See basement waterproofing. If the water has a sewage smell, call immediately, because a sewer backup is a different problem. See sewer line cleaning.

Testing your pump before the wet season

Once or twice a year, pour a few gallons of water into the pit and watch the pump start, run and shut off. Check that the discharge runs clear outside and does not freeze in winter. Listen for rattles or grinding, and look for debris in the pit.

If the pump takes too long to start or does not stop, replace the float. If it vibrates, check the impeller. A short test is easier than a flooded basement, and it tells you whether to schedule service before the storms arrive.
